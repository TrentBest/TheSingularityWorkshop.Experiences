# Civilization Interface and Perception

**Status:** candidate design direction. The principles below guide exploration; species-specific perception models and any associated simulation contracts remain to be defined.

## The central idea

A civilization does not encounter reality as a neutral list of facts. It encounters reality through a body, substrate, sensory range, timescale, memory, culture, instruments, and learned ways of interpreting evidence.

A human pilot sees a warning light. A crystalline intelligence may experience the same structural fault as a spreading interruption in a field of harmonic color. A distributed machine civilization may perceive it as a sudden loss of reachable relationships across a network. An oceanic organism may register pressure, chemistry, and current long before it has a concept corresponding to a visible object.

These are not different universes. They are different, potentially incomplete ways of encountering one shared universe.

**The same event can be objectively consequential, differently perceived, differently interpreted, and differently remembered.**

Galactic War should make that difference playable. The visitor should sometimes discover that another civilization is not hiding information; it may genuinely lack the sensory concepts, categories, or timescale needed to notice what a human considers obvious. The reverse should be equally true.

## Four layers that must not be confused

A perception system should keep four layers distinct:

1. **World state:** what the authoritative world model records as having happened, including relevant physical, social, and institutional facts.
2. **Perception:** what an entity or observer can detect from its location, body, instruments, communication channels, attention, and current condition.
3. **Interpretation:** what that observer believes the detected evidence means, using its knowledge, culture, models, and assumptions.
4. **Presentation:** how an Experience renders or communicates that perception and interpretation to a visitor through sound, image, text, haptics, spatial structure, or another modality.

A presentation is not automatically the world state. A character's interpretation is not automatically true. An observer can miss a fact, detect it too late, misclassify it, or correctly detect something but misunderstand its cause.

This separation protects both simulation integrity and narrative possibility. It also makes uncertainty meaningful rather than a decorative fog effect.

## Perception is a capability, not a universal camera

A civilization's perception profile can be explored through several dimensions:

- **Substrate:** biological senses, chemical networks, crystalline resonance, electromagnetic fields, mechanical sensors, computation, or combinations.
- **Channels:** light, sound, pressure, temperature, vibration, radio, magnetic fields, chemical gradients, particle flux, timing, and signals not yet understood.
- **Range and resolution:** what can be detected, from how far away, with what precision, and at what cost.
- **Timescale:** milliseconds, human-like moments, seasonal cycles, geological intervals, or parallel windows of attention.
- **Spatial model:** point-like objects, continuous fields, topology, gradients, nested spaces, relationships, or distributed locations.
- **Attention:** what is salient, what is ignored, and what competes for limited processing or awareness.
- **Memory:** what is retained, compressed, shared, reconstructed, or deliberately allowed to decay.
- **Interpretive vocabulary:** which distinctions the observer can make and which concepts must be learned or translated.
- **Blind spots:** conditions under which the observer becomes unreliable, overwhelmed, or unable to distinguish signal from noise.

These dimensions should not become a menu of arbitrary bonuses. They should explain what an observer can know and why.

## Civilization-specific interfaces

A civilization's interface is the practical bridge between its way of perceiving and its ability to act. It may be biological, cultural, technological, architectural, or all four.

For humans, a ship's engineering interface might turn vibration, temperature, pressure, and power flow into gauges, diagrams, alarms, and physical controls. Those abstractions are useful, but they discard detail. A skilled engineer may learn to hear a bearing failing before the diagnostic system reports it.

For a crystalline civilization, structural stress might be directly experienced as a change in color, resonance, or spatial coherence. Its built environment could expose load paths as naturally as a human building exposes corridors. A human visitor might need an instrument to perceive what local inhabitants notice effortlessly.

For a distributed intelligence, a map may not primarily represent geography. It may show reachable processes, dependencies, trust, latency, and the health of relationships. Two physically distant nodes could feel adjacent because they exchange information continuously; a nearby isolated node could feel impossibly remote.

For an oceanic civilization, currents and chemical plumes might carry the equivalent of public announcements, historical traces, and emotional signals. A dry sealed chamber could be not merely uncomfortable but informationally silent.

These are design examples, not established canon. Their purpose is to push interfaces beyond reskinned human dashboards.

## GUI as a family of perspectives

The GUI stack should be treated as a way to compose meaningful views over shared domain state—not as the authority that defines the state.

One ship, station, organism, moon, or event may have several legitimate representations:

| Perspective | What it emphasizes |
|---|---|
| Embodied | What a visitor can sense and manipulate from a particular body or location |
| Instrumental | Measurements, warnings, controls, and diagnostic evidence |
| Engineering | Components, dependencies, flows, constraints, faults, and repair options |
| Scientific | Observations, uncertainty, hypotheses, comparisons, and provenance |
| Social | Relationships, roles, obligations, reputation, and conflicting accounts |
| Strategic | Routes, resources, influence, readiness, and large-scale consequences |
| Ecological | Flows, habitats, interdependence, stress, and long-term change |
| Historical | Events, memory, records, competing interpretations, and change over time |

A perspective is a purposeful projection, not a separate copy of the universe. Switching from strategic view to embodied view should not silently create a different ship or reset its condition. A repair made through an engineering interface should be reflected in the embodied world because both views refer to the same underlying entity and authoritative change.

The interface can expose only what its observer is entitled and able to know. A strategic map should not reveal a hidden enemy simply because the renderer can draw it. A scientific view should distinguish measured evidence from inferred structure. A social view should distinguish a recorded promise from a promise that was kept.

## Translation is not equivalence

When observers communicate across different sensory and conceptual systems, translation should preserve uncertainty.

A translator may be able to say:
- "This signal is probably an invitation."
- "The closest human analogy is a warning, but the source may not distinguish warning from boundary."
- "The pattern indicates a recurring obligation, not ownership."
- "We can detect a change in the moon's thermal field, but we do not know whether it is intentional."

The interface should not turn an approximation into a falsely exact sentence. It may present confidence, alternative interpretations, missing context, and the evidence behind a translation.

This is especially important when negotiation crosses incompatible concepts. One civilization may describe a migration corridor as a living obligation; another may describe it as unused navigable space. Both can be internally consistent while reaching incompatible conclusions. Translation can make the disagreement understandable without pretending to resolve it.

## Perception can change through learning and relationship

An observer's perception need not be fixed at character creation.

A pilot learns the sound of a damaged engine. A scientist discovers that a moon's slow thermal pulses form a repeatable language. A ship's intelligence learns the crew's routines and begins presenting anomalies in ways they can recognize. A symbiotic partner develops a shared vocabulary with a host. A civilization gradually builds instruments that let it perceive a phenomenon previously outside its range.

Learning can therefore unlock more than new controls. It can reveal new categories of evidence.

Relationships can also alter what is noticed. A moon intelligence that has learned a ship's engine rhythm may recognize its approach before conventional sensors identify it. A station's inhabitants may notice the visitor's insignia and infer a history that the visitor does not know they are carrying.

These changes must be grounded in world events and acquired knowledge. The interface must not grant omniscience merely because the visitor has progressed.

## The living moon: a perception example

Imagine a moon whose global intelligence experiences its crust as a distributed sensory and computational structure.

A mining crew sees a valuable seam and a set of stable geological readings. Local inhabitants experience the excavation as a persistent interruption in a familiar resonance. A regional intelligence detects that several distant structures are losing synchronization. The moon's deeper cognition may not respond for days—or may have already incorporated the disturbance into a decision that local minds cannot understand.

A human visitor might first see ordinary geological telemetry. After collecting samples, listening to local accounts, and learning the resonance pattern, the visitor gains access to a new instrument view. The same earlier event now becomes legible in a different way.

Nothing about the past event changed. The visitor's ability to perceive and interpret it changed.

The moon may also misread the visitor. It might interpret repeated engine pulses as an aggressive drilling pattern, then gradually learn that the rhythm is a greeting used by the ship's crew. Both sides change their models of the other.

## Implementation boundaries in the Workshop stack

This design should compose existing Workshop capabilities rather than create a parallel presentation or simulation framework.

- **Experiences** owns Galactic War's meaning: which civilizations perceive what, what their interfaces mean, and which interpretations or rules are specific to this universe.
- **FSM_API** can represent persistent entities, observer state, learning state, and meaningful transitions. It does not define the fictional sensory model by itself.
- **FSM_COS** composes the capabilities required by an observer, instrument, environment, or interface.
- **FSM_Foundry** executes composed behavior. A capability description and its execution remain distinct.
- **Physics** owns reusable physical and mathematical models. Fictional sensory channels must not be presented as validated physical models until they are actually specified and implemented.
- **GUI.Core** supplies reusable visual meaning and interaction structures. Civilization-specific views should be composed from it rather than embedded as universal assumptions in the GUI framework.
- **Renderer** and **Visualization** are consumed as MicroBundles. They provide manifestations of state and perspective; they do not own authoritative world state.
- **GrammarAi** and **ProtocolAi** may support vocabulary, signal structures, and communication protocols. They should not silently claim that semantic understanding has been achieved merely because a signal was parsed.
- **DataWarehouse** can preserve observations, evidence, learned associations, and historical records according to its own boundaries. An observation is not automatically an authoritative mutation.

A future perception contract may need to describe an observer identity, available channels, range and resolution, current conditions, attention, knowledge, uncertainty, and the provenance of each observation. This document does not prescribe a final schema or runtime API.

## Design rules

1. **One world, many perspectives.** Different views must remain projections of shared entities and events.
2. **Perception is situated.** Every observation has an observer, context, channel, and limitation.
3. **Do not leak omniscience.** A presentation can show only what its source perspective can know, unless it is explicitly an out-of-world authoring or debugging tool.
4. **Separate evidence from belief.** Preserve the difference between what was measured, what was inferred, and what someone claims.
5. **Make uncertainty legible.** Translation and interpretation should expose ambiguity rather than hide it behind confident UI.
6. **Let learning reveal reality.** New knowledge can change what becomes perceptible without changing the underlying event.
7. **Preserve alien difference.** Do not translate every civilization into human senses, human ownership, human time, or human interface conventions.
8. **Keep accessibility distinct from flattening.** Provide alternate ways for the visitor to understand and interact with information without claiming that every fictional species experiences it identically.
9. **Keep presentation non-authoritative.** A visual effect, map, or interaction control is not proof that a world event occurred.
10. **Let relationships change perception.** Trust, familiarity, shared history, and learned signals can become real sources of knowledge.

## Questions requiring illumination

- Which civilizations have genuinely non-human sensory models, and which are primarily cultural or technological variations?
- Can a visitor temporarily experience another being's perception, or must it always be mediated through instruments and translation?
- How should the Experience communicate a sensory channel that has no direct human equivalent?
- Which observations are private, shareable, recorded, or politically sensitive?
- Can an observer deliberately deceive another observer by shaping what it can perceive?
- How do distributed and long-timescale intelligences express attention, interruption, urgency, and consent?
- What does a shared interface look like when several species must operate the same machine?
- Which first vertical slice can prove that multiple perspectives remain consistent over one authoritative event?

## A promising first experiment

Use one consequential but understandable event: a mining tool damages a sensory structure on the living moon.

Present that same event through three perspectives:
1. a human engineer's diagnostic interface;
2. a local symbiont's resonance-based perception;
3. a regional intelligence's network-level awareness.

Each view should reveal some evidence, omit other evidence, and support a different initial interpretation. The visitor gathers observations and compares them. A later discovery should reconcile at least part of the disagreement without making every observer foolish or dishonest.

The experiment succeeds if the visitor can understand that all three perspectives refer to one event, recognize why each observer interpreted it differently, and make a consequential decision based on evidence rather than a privileged omniscient map.

The goal is not to build three skins for the same dashboard. It is to make **the act of understanding another way of experiencing reality** one of the defining adventures of Galactic War.
