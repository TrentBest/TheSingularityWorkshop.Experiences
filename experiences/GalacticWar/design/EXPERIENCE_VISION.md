# Galactic War — Experience Vision

**Status:** concept and candidate design. This document captures intent; it does not claim an implemented universe.

## The world promise

A far-future civilization inhabits a universe where technology has advanced far enough to feel magical. The setting should support extraordinary heroes and villains, immense machines, strange phenomena, and conflicts whose scale can threaten worlds. The central enemy or opposing force may have overwhelming numbers; survival, ingenuity, alliances, and hard choices therefore matter.

The universe must remain internally coherent. The audience should be able to wonder how something works, investigate it, and gradually understand its rules. The word *magic* describes the intended sense of wonder; whether specific phenomena are technological, physical, or genuinely supernatural remains an explicit worldbuilding decision.

## Learning before adulthood

Children encounter the systems of their civilization early through discovery, play, guided experimentation, and age-appropriate learning. They can explore machines, energy, materials, navigation, biology, history, culture, and the institutions around them.

This is preparation for future agency—not a declaration that children are combatants or that military service is the inevitable destination. The world should offer many meaningful futures: engineering, research, medicine, construction, exploration, diplomacy, logistics, governance, creative work, and, for those who choose it and qualify, military service.

The visitor should experience the appeal of learning because knowledge unlocks new ways to understand and participate in the world.

## Heroes, villains, and ordinary people

Extraordinary characters should emerge from the world rather than replace it. Heroes have limits, obligations, relationships, and consequences. Villains have motives and strategies that can be understood even when their actions are opposed. Neither label should flatten the cultures and people caught between them.

A galaxy feels alive when ordinary people have lives beyond the conflict: communities, families, work, commerce, art, celebrations, institutions, disagreements, and local histories. The stakes of a mission become legible because the visitor can see who and what may be changed by its outcome.

## A world that can be explored

The visitor should be able to follow a question through layers:
- a person and their immediate circumstances;
- the equipment and systems they depend on;
- the team, institution, or faction around them;
- the world and its communities;
- the interstellar network of ships, fleets, alliances, and competing powers.

History should be discoverable through people, archives, locations, and conflicting accounts. Do not assume a single exposition source is complete or unbiased.

## Design constraints

- Avoid treating present-day military or political structures as universal defaults for a far-future civilization.
- Avoid using technology as arbitrary spectacle: important systems need understandable constraints and consequences.
- Avoid making every story path a combat path.
- Keep fictional physics distinct from established Physics implementations until explicitly modelled and validated.
- Keep the visitor's perspective and agency clear at every scale.
- Preserve the difference between authored lore, simulated state, and illustrative presentation.

## Questions requiring illumination

- What is the nature of the overwhelming opposing force, and what makes it difficult to defeat?
- Is the universe's magical dimension literal, technological, or a deliberate combination?
- What is the first world, institution, or community the visitor encounters?
- Which social structures make early learning natural without making the world feel like a school simulator?
- How do the setting's extraordinary heroes and villains intersect with ordinary people's lives?
