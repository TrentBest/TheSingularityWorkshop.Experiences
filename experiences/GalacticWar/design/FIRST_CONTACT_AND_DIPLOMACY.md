# First Contact and Diplomacy

**Status:** candidate design direction. This document explores contact as a process of discovery, interpretation, negotiation, and consequence. It does not establish a universal first-contact protocol or declare any example civilization canon.

## Contact is not a conversation

Two civilizations can occupy the same place, exchange signals, and still fail to make contact in any meaningful sense.

A signal may be noise to its receiver. A repeated pattern may be mistaken for weather, machinery, aggression, or an accidental by-product. A reply may be received but interpreted as a threat. A message may be understood perfectly while its intention remains unknown.

Galactic War should treat first contact as a chain of uncertain achievements, not a dialogue box that appears when two ships enter range.

A useful distinction:

1. **Detection:** something unusual is observed.
2. **Attribution:** an observer suspects that the phenomenon has an agent or source.
3. **Intentionality:** the observer finds evidence that the source is acting deliberately.
4. **Address:** one side forms a signal or action intended for the other.
5. **Recognition:** the other side recognizes that the signal is addressed to it.
6. **Reciprocity:** both sides establish a repeatable exchange.
7. **Interpretation:** they build provisional models of what the exchange means.
8. **Relationship:** they establish expectations, boundaries, obligations, or a decision not to continue.

These stages can overlap, reverse, or remain unresolved. A civilization may recognize an address without understanding its content. It may understand content but reject the premise that the sender has authority to make the request.

## The first question may be: what counts as an individual?

Human diplomatic assumptions often begin with an individual representative speaking for a polity. That model is not universal.

The source of contact could be:

- one organism acting independently;
- a family, colony, or temporary collective;
- a regional organ of a larger intelligence;
- a machine process representing a distributed network;
- a settlement whose inhabitants disagree about whether they are part of the same civilization;
- an ecosystem whose responses emerge from many species and feedback loops;
- an institution authorized to speak only about a narrow responsibility;
- a long-lived intelligence whose reply is assembled across generations.

A visitor must not infer that the first responsive entity speaks for everyone. Nor should a civilization be treated as a single mind merely because its members share a substrate or history.

Diplomacy begins by learning who—or what—can make which commitments.

## Contact channels and shared experiments

Contact should begin with observable behavior, not an assumed universal language.

Potential channels include:

- repeated light, sound, radio, or electromagnetic patterns;
- changes in temperature, pressure, chemistry, or flow;
- synchronized movement or changes in spatial arrangement;
- exchange of objects, energy, or carefully bounded resources;
- repeated environmental actions that create a recognizable pattern;
- machine-readable structures, provided both sides understand the conventions;
- long-period signals that may require patience and persistent observation.

The most promising early exchange may be a shared experiment. One side changes a harmless, reversible condition; the other responds; both observe whether the response is repeatable.

A useful experiment has:
- a clear baseline;
- a small and bounded change;
- a way to distinguish response from coincidence;
- an agreed or inferred stopping condition;
- a record of what each side actually observed;
- a plan for what to do if the result is ambiguous.

The visitor should be able to choose restraint. Not every unknown signal needs an immediate reply, and not every silence is hostility.

## A contact ladder, not a universal handshake

A civilization may recognize several forms of contact without treating them as equivalent.

| Contact state | What has been established | What remains unknown |
|---|---|---|
| Unclassified phenomenon | An anomaly exists | Whether it has an agent |
| Possible agency | Some behavior appears organized | Whether it is intentional |
| Possible address | A pattern appears directed | Whether it is meant for this observer |
| Recognized exchange | A repeatable response exists | What the responses mean |
| Provisional translation | Some meanings have plausible interpretations | Whether the concepts align |
| Bounded agreement | Specific actions or limits are understood well enough to test | Whether the agreement generalizes |
| Relationship | History and expectations now shape future contact | Whether trust will endure |

This is a diagnostic model for the Experience, not a mandatory progression tree. A relationship can begin through shared work or mutual rescue without a formal language. A long-established trade relationship can still contain deep semantic misunderstandings.

## Translation should carry its evidence

A translated message should never appear as an unqualified fact when its meaning is uncertain.

A useful translation record may contain:

- the original signal or observation;
- the source and receiving observer;
- channel, location, and conditions;
- the interpretation currently offered;
- alternative interpretations;
- confidence and the basis for that confidence;
- the translator, instrument, or learned model involved;
- what evidence would change the interpretation;
- whether the source has confirmed the interpretation.

For example:

> **Working interpretation:** “Do not cross.”
>
> **Alternative:** “Cross only while the current is flowing outward.”
>
> **Evidence:** The signal repeats at the boundary and changes with current direction.
>
> **Unknown:** Whether the boundary is territorial, ecological, hazardous, or ceremonial.

This is more useful than a perfectly fluent but unjustifiably certain subtitle. The visitor can decide what risk to take and what question to ask next.

A translation model can become more capable through experience, but its confidence should be earned. Parsing a pattern is not proof of semantic understanding; recognizing a repeated association is not proof of shared intent.

## Diplomacy is negotiation across incompatible models

The most difficult disagreements may not be about facts. They may be about categories.

One civilization may understand a migration corridor as an inherited obligation to living descendants. Another may classify it as open navigable space. One may regard a memory-bearing structure as a person; another may regard it as salvage. One may interpret silence as respect; another as refusal.

Diplomacy should expose the underlying models:
- What entities are recognized?
- What can be owned, shared, borrowed, repaired, or moved?
- Who can consent, and on whose behalf?
- Which obligations persist across generations?
- What counts as harm?
- Which resources are replaceable?
- What does a promise bind: an individual, a collective, a territory, or a future process?
- How is disagreement represented inside each society?

The Experience should not reduce every conflict to a hidden correct answer. Sometimes the visitor discovers a factual misunderstanding. Sometimes the parties genuinely value different things. Sometimes a supposed representative lacks authority. Sometimes no agreement is possible without changing the conditions that created the dispute.

## Consent, boundaries, and the right not to engage

Contact is an action with consequences. The visitor may expose a location, introduce a pathogen or contaminant, alter an ecosystem, trigger a defense, or create obligations neither side understands.

The design should distinguish:
- permission to observe;
- permission to approach;
- permission to communicate;
- permission to enter;
- permission to sample or modify;
- permission to share information;
- permission to represent another party;
- permission to make a lasting agreement.

One permission must not silently imply the others.

A refusal may be local, temporary, conditional, or issued by an entity that lacks authority over the whole region. The visitor should investigate carefully without treating every boundary as an obstacle to overcome. A civilization's right to remain unknown is a legitimate outcome.

The visitor also needs boundaries. A first-contact scenario should allow the crew to withdraw, quarantine, decline an exchange, or ask for time without automatically being marked cowardly or hostile.

## Power asymmetry and the danger of accidental empire

A technologically advanced visitor can alter another society simply by arriving.

A gift may destabilize a local economy. A medical intervention may change population dynamics. A translated legal concept may privilege one faction over another. A powerful sensor may reveal private information. A repair may remove a natural constraint that shaped an ecosystem for millennia.

The visitor's intentions do not erase these effects.

First-contact scenarios should therefore track:
- differences in technological and material power;
- dependency created by aid or trade;
- who receives benefits and who bears costs;
- local factions and dissent;
- knowledge revealed or withheld;
- ecological and cultural externalities;
- whether a decision can be reversed;
- whether the visitor has created an expectation it cannot sustain.

The goal is not to make every intervention dangerous. It is to make consequences discoverable and proportionate to the intervention.

## A worked encounter: the boundary that answers

A ship discovers a region of apparently empty space around a small, slow-moving moon. Navigation probes repeatedly drift away from a narrow corridor. No weapon fire or conventional warning is detected.

**First observation:** The corridor's boundary shifts in a regular pattern. The crew records it as an unexplained navigation anomaly.

**Attribution:** Repeated measurements reveal that the shifts respond to probe trajectories. The crew suspects an active system but cannot tell whether it is artificial, biological, or ecological.

**First address:** The crew sends a low-energy probe along a path that stops short of the boundary. The boundary changes shape, leaving the probe untouched.

**Recognition experiment:** The crew repeats the approach with a different trajectory and then withdraws. The boundary produces a corresponding pattern. A repeatable exchange is emerging, but its meaning is unknown.

**Competing interpretations:** The ship's computer classifies the behavior as perimeter defense. A local symbiont interprets it as a request to slow down. A regional intelligence may be attempting to route the ship away from a damaged habitat. None of these interpretations is yet confirmed.

**Discovery:** The crew learns that the corridor is a migration path for fragile organisms. The boundary is not a wall around territory; it is a temporary protective flow that changes with the migration cycle.

**Decision:** The visitor can wait, negotiate a timed crossing, help repair a damaged migration segment, or leave. Each choice changes what the region can infer about the ship.

**Consequence:** The encounter does not end with a universal greeting. It establishes a specific shared history: this ship approached, observed, tested, listened, and either respected or disrupted a living route.

The encounter succeeds when the visitor understands more than the initial sensor reading—not when every mystery has been translated into human language.

## Mission patterns that emerge from first contact

- **The Wrong Recipient:** A message is addressed to a regional process, but the visitor replies to a local node that cannot accept the commitment.
- **The Gift That Cannot Be Returned:** A seemingly harmless gift creates a dependency or obligation the receiving society cannot easily refuse.
- **A Thousand-Year Reply:** A signal sent by an earlier expedition receives an answer after the original crew and their polity have changed.
- **The Silent Majority:** A visible representative agrees to a treaty, while other members of the distributed civilization do not recognize that representative's authority.
- **The Boundary Is Alive:** A navigation restriction is a protective behavior of an ecosystem, not a political border.
- **The Translation Dispute:** Two interpreters agree on every signal pattern but disagree about the underlying concept.
- **No Contact Requested:** The visitor discovers evidence of intelligence and must decide whether to respect a deliberate refusal to engage.

These patterns should support investigation, patience, negotiation, retreat, and sometimes combat. Combat may resolve an immediate threat; it rarely resolves the meaning of the encounter.

## Architecture and implementation boundaries

First contact is an Experience-level domain, composed from shared Workshop capabilities.

- **Experiences** owns the encounter, its fictional entities, interpretations, diplomacy rules, and consequences.
- **FSM_API** can represent persistent participants, contact states, commitments, and transitions. It does not decide what a signal means.
- **FSM_COS** composes the capabilities available to observers, translators, instruments, and representatives.
- **FSM_Foundry** executes the composed processes. Authorization to propose an action remains distinct from execution of that action.
- **Physics** owns reusable physical models for channels and environmental effects. Fictional signals must not be passed off as validated physics.
- **GUI.Core** can compose observation, evidence, uncertainty, translation, and negotiation interfaces. A view must not grant knowledge unavailable to its observer.
- **Renderer** and **Visualization** remain MicroBundle-consumed manifestations, not authoritative diplomatic state.
- **GrammarAi** and **ProtocolAi** may help identify structure, syntax, and protocol patterns. A valid parse does not establish shared meaning or consent.
- **DataWarehouse** can preserve observations, translation revisions, commitments, and encounter history with provenance.
- **Economy** may describe exchange, resource rights, and obligations where relevant. A negotiated access right or cultural obligation should not be flattened into a price.

A future encounter model may distinguish participant identity, representation authority, observations, signals, interpretations, translation hypotheses, permissions, commitments, and resulting events. This document deliberately does not prescribe a final schema or runtime API.

## Design rules

1. **Detection is not understanding.** Keep each achievement in contact distinguishable.
2. **A responder is not automatically a representative.** Establish scope and authority.
3. **Silence is ambiguous.** It may mean absence, delay, refusal, inability, or a different timescale.
4. **Preserve source evidence.** Keep original observations available when translations change.
5. **Represent uncertainty honestly.** Never make an approximation look exact merely to simplify the interface.
6. **Consent is scoped.** Observation, approach, entry, sampling, exchange, and agreement are separate permissions.
7. **Respect non-engagement.** Contact is not an entitlement.
8. **Model internal plurality.** Civilizations contain factions, local interests, and disagreements.
9. **Track asymmetric consequences.** Good intentions do not guarantee harmless outcomes.
10. **Allow reversible choices where possible.** Withdrawal and restraint are meaningful actions.
11. **Do not force a diplomatic ending.** Some encounters remain unresolved.
12. **Let shared history matter.** Future contact should reflect what each participant actually experienced and remembers.

## Questions requiring illumination

- How does a distributed intelligence authorize a representative?
- What forms of consent are meaningful for an ecosystem or a planetary-scale mind?
- How should the Experience handle contact across radically different timescales?
- Can two civilizations share a protocol while retaining incompatible meanings?
- What is the smallest persistent record needed to make a relationship feel continuous?
- How should the visitor discover that a gift, repair, or rescue has created an unintended obligation?
- Which signals should be perceivable through ordinary instruments, and which require learned or borrowed sensory capabilities?
- When should a civilization's refusal remain opaque rather than becoming a puzzle to solve?

## The intended feeling

First contact should feel like approaching a door that may not be a door, in a house whose inhabitants may not agree that it is a house.

The visitor brings instruments, assumptions, needs, and power. The other side brings its own history, categories, vulnerabilities, and reasons to respond—or not.

The adventure is not simply learning how to speak to aliens. It is learning how to meet another form of existence without assuming that the universe has been waiting to be addressed.
