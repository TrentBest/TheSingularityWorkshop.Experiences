# Lived-In Ships and Viewports

**Status:** candidate experience direction.

## A ship is a place

The visitor should not experience every spacecraft as a floating camera with a weapon attached. They are occupying a physical vessel. Its viewport, hull, instruments, rooms, machinery, crew, and movement establish a spatial relationship between the person and the vehicle.

The experience scales with the ship. A one-person fighter can be cockpit-centered and still feel embodied. A larger vessel can become a navigable environment with its own routines, inhabitants, and places.

## Viewport-first presence

For a small craft, the primary view is through its actual viewport or canopy. The player sees space through glass and structure, with the frame, reflections, lighting, instruments, and hull geometry communicating that they are inside the craft.

The viewport is not merely a decorative overlay around a conventional camera. It is part of the physical scene and should make orientation, visibility, damage, and spatial scale legible.

## From cockpit to interior

As vessels become larger, the experience can reveal more of the ship:
- cockpit and pilot stations;
- accessible systems and maintenance points;
- corridors and rooms;
- crew quarters and shared spaces;
- engineering, power, sensor, and propulsion spaces;
- hangars, cargo areas, briefing spaces, and command stations.

The ship's layout should follow its purpose, size, technology, crew, and culture. Not every vessel needs every room, and a small craft should not be burdened with a capital-ship interior.

## Walking while traveling

On sufficiently large vessels, the visitor can leave the pilot or control station and move around inside while the ship continues its journey. They can speak with people, inspect or operate systems, prepare equipment, review information, rest, socialize, and participate in shipboard life.

The vessel remains a moving object in the authoritative world simulation. Interior movement is a perspective and interaction within that vessel, not a pause in its travel. Travel time can become meaningful lived time rather than a loading screen or an empty wait.

The experience should make transitions clear: piloting, riding as a passenger, walking aboard, using a remote station, and observing are distinct modes with distinct permissions and consequences.

## Spatial and simulation boundaries

- The ship's world position, orientation, velocity, and condition belong to authoritative vessel state.
- The visitor's position inside the vessel is relative to a defined ship interior frame.
- Ship motion and interior movement must remain coherent; acceleration, rotation, damage, and transitions should not produce unexplained displacement.
- Interior spaces are manifestations of the vessel's actual layout and systems, not unrelated scenes.
- Stations and interactable systems expose capabilities through explicit permissions and state.
- Crew, NPCs, and other players occupy the same authored spatial model where the Experience supports shared presence.

## Long-distance travel as an experience

A long journey can contain preparation, conversations, maintenance, training, discoveries, unexpected encounters, and social life. The ship becomes a mobile home and operational base. The visitor should be able to make use of the time without requiring constant combat or arbitrary interruptions.

- Which ship systems are physically accessible, and which are represented through remote interfaces?
- How are crew schedules, NPC behavior, and multiplayer occupancy represented?
- How does shipboard time relate to galaxy travel, time dilation, and the setting's faster-than-light rules?


## Docking and station interfaces

Docking connects a moving vessel to a larger inhabited infrastructure. It should feel like an arrival into a place, not a teleport to a service screen.

A small fighter may receive a narrow approach corridor, align with a compact berth, and settle into a magnetic or mechanical clamp. A shuttle may enter a pressurized hangar under local traffic guidance. A freighter may align with a cargo collar, connect transfer lines, and coordinate unloading. A capital ship may need to approach at controlled speed and orientation while station controllers, tug craft, beacon systems, guidance crews, and enormous clamps bring it into a secure berth.

The vessel class and berth determine the docking procedure. Relevant factors can include:
- approach corridor, traffic priority, clearance envelope, and local control instructions;
- vessel dimensions, mass, momentum, maneuverability, and available thrusters;
- berth geometry, clamp placement, magnetic capture, cradle supports, and hull access;
- pressure, atmosphere, temperature, radiation, and other environmental compatibility;
- power, fuel, data, cargo, passenger, and maintenance connections;
- security, identification, customs, quarantine, and restricted-area permissions;
- damage, emergencies, station condition, and the consequences of an unsafe approach.

The player should be able to choose the right level of involvement. Routine docking can be guided or automated, while manual docking, unfamiliar stations, damaged systems, contested ports, unusual vessel sizes, or emergency arrivals can demand active piloting and coordination. Automation should be visible and understandable rather than a magical cut.

Docking is also an opportunity for spectacle and scale. A fighter can dart through traffic and settle into a berth; a capital ship can dominate the viewport as it slowly enters a docking cradle, with clamps closing across its hull and service craft moving around it. The station's response should make the arrival feel consequential: traffic may be rerouted, crews mobilize, public displays update, and visitors gather to watch an important vessel arrive.

## Stations are places, not menus

Stations can function as ports, cities, shipyards, markets, schools, research centers, diplomatic venues, military bases, refuges, or industrial complexes. Their purpose should shape their architecture, population, traffic, services, security, and local culture.

A station's districts and systems can include:
- docking rings, hangars, cargo collars, passenger terminals, and customs;
- public promenades, markets, food halls, entertainment, and observation galleries;
- repair yards, workshops, fabrication facilities, warehouses, and logistics offices;
- schools, academies, libraries, laboratories, and training simulators;
- medical services, residential areas, crew facilities, and places of worship or reflection;
- command centers, security posts, diplomatic suites, and restricted infrastructure;
- hidden, abandoned, damaged, or contested spaces that reveal its history.

The visitor should be able to arrive for one purpose and discover several others. A repair visit can lead to a conversation, a school demonstration, a local dispute, an unusual ship, a rumor about an adversary, or a community event. Not every interaction needs to become a quest. The station should be interesting simply to inhabit.

Stations should have independent rhythms: shift changes, arrivals and departures, market cycles, maintenance windows, public events, shortages, celebrations, security alerts, and changes in control. Their services depend on people, supply, power, infrastructure, and political conditions. A blockade or damaged docking ring should change the lived experience, not just a status label.

## Docking as a social transition

The passage from ship to station is an experience boundary with physical and social meaning. Depending on the technology and culture, it may involve an airlock, docking tube, gangway, hangar floor, passenger bridge, pressure equalization, environmental checks, identity verification, customs, quarantine, or local access rules.

The visitor should understand what is happening and why. Procedures can vary by station, faction, vessel, and situation. A trusted local pilot may receive a streamlined welcome; an unknown freighter may be inspected; a capital ship carrying a powerful figure may trigger ceremony, security, political attention, or public excitement.

The station should remember arrivals and departures where they matter. A vessel's reputation, ownership, markings, recent actions, or faction affiliation can influence clearance, service, welcome, suspicion, or denial. These responses should follow explicit world rules rather than arbitrary scripted hostility.
