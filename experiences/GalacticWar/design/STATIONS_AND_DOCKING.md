# Space Stations and Docking

**Status:** candidate design direction.

Docking connects a moving vessel to a larger inhabited infrastructure. It should feel like an arrival into a place, not a teleport to a service screen.

A small fighter may receive a narrow approach corridor, align with a compact berth, and settle into a magnetic or mechanical clamp. A shuttle may enter a pressurized hangar under local traffic guidance. A freighter may align with a cargo collar, connect transfer lines, and coordinate unloading. A capital ship may need to approach at controlled speed and orientation while station controllers, tug craft, beacon systems, guidance crews, and enormous clamps bring it into a secure berth.

The vessel class and berth determine the docking procedure. Relevant factors can include:
- approach corridor, traffic priority, clearance envelope, and local control instructions;
- vessel dimensions, mass, momentum, maneuverability, and available thrusters;
- berth geometry, clamp placement, magnetic capture, cradle supports, and hull access;
- pressure, atmosphere, temperature, radiation, and other environmental compatibility;
- power, fuel, data, cargo, passenger, and maintenance connections;
- security, identification, customs, quarantine, and restricted-area permissions;
- damage, emergencies, station condition, and the consequences of an unsafe approach.

The player should be able to choose the right level of involvement. Routine docking can be guided or automated, while manual docking, unfamiliar stations, damaged systems, contested ports, unusual vessel sizes, or emergency arrivals can demand active piloting and coordination. Automation should be visible and understandable rather than a magical cut.

Docking is also an opportunity for spectacle and scale. A fighter can dart through traffic and settle into a berth; a capital ship can dominate the viewport as it slowly enters a docking cradle, with clamps closing across its hull and service craft moving around it. The station's response should make the arrival feel consequential: traffic may be rerouted, crews mobilize, public displays update, and visitors gather to watch an important vessel arrive.

## Stations are places, not menus

Stations can function as ports, cities, shipyards, markets, schools, research centers, diplomatic venues, military bases, refuges, or industrial complexes. Their purpose should shape their architecture, population, traffic, services, security, and local culture.

A station's districts and systems can include:
- docking rings, hangars, cargo collars, passenger terminals, and customs;
- public promenades, markets, food halls, entertainment, and observation galleries;
- repair yards, workshops, fabrication facilities, warehouses, and logistics offices;
- schools, academies, libraries, laboratories, and training simulators;
- medical services, residential areas, crew facilities, and places of worship or reflection;
- command centers, security posts, diplomatic suites, and restricted infrastructure;
- hidden, abandoned, damaged, or contested spaces that reveal its history.

The visitor should be able to arrive for one purpose and discover several others. A repair visit can lead to a conversation, a school demonstration, a local dispute, an unusual ship, a rumor about an adversary, or a community event. Not every interaction needs to become a quest. The station should be interesting simply to inhabit.

Stations should have independent rhythms: shift changes, arrivals and departures, market cycles, maintenance windows, public events, shortages, celebrations, security alerts, and changes in control. Their services depend on people, supply, power, infrastructure, and political conditions. A blockade or damaged docking ring should change the lived experience, not just a status label.

## A visit has a rhythm

A station visit can unfold as a chain of connected, optional experiences:

1. **Approach:** identify the station, read its traffic, recognize its scale and condition, and learn what arrival procedures apply.
2. **Clearance:** communicate with traffic control, receive a berth, declare cargo or passengers where required, and respond to local rules.
3. **Docking:** perform, assist, delegate, or observe the vessel-specific maneuver.
4. **Disembarkation:** cross the physical connection, pass through any environmental or security boundary, and enter the station as a visitor or resident.
5. **Orientation:** discover the station's layout, services, public spaces, people, and current circumstances.
6. **Participation:** repair, trade, learn, work, meet people, explore, attend an event, pursue a lead, or simply enjoy the place.
7. **Departure:** settle services and obligations, obtain clearance, return to the vessel, and depart into a changed traffic environment.

This is a useful design rhythm, not a compulsory checklist. A fighter pilot on a quick turnaround should not have to tour the station. A visitor who wants to spend an evening in a station city should have far more to discover than a repair menu.

The station should communicate where the visitor is, what is available, and what is restricted without turning every doorway into a blocking interface. Signage, architecture, public announcements, maps, local guides, visible queues, uniforms, and behavior can all teach the place.

## A station has an operating identity

A station should be recognizable through more than its name and visual theme. Its operating identity emerges from the combination of:
- **Purpose:** why it exists and what it contributes to its region.
- **People:** who lives, works, studies, visits, governs, maintains, and depends on it.
- **Layout:** how its districts connect and which groups can access them.
- **Economy:** what it produces, imports, exports, repairs, teaches, and struggles to obtain.
- **Culture:** customs, language, food, architecture, dress, celebrations, etiquette, and shared memory.
- **Governance:** who sets rules, how decisions are made, and where authority is contested.
- **Condition:** age, maintenance, damage, expansion, overcrowding, prosperity, or decline.
- **Connections:** routes, neighbors, suppliers, rivals, institutions, and political commitments.

These dimensions should reinforce one another. A remote repair station may have improvised additions, a close-knit crew, scarce luxury goods, and deep expertise in keeping obsolete vessels operational. A prestigious academy station may have elegant public spaces, demanding standards, alumni networks, and tension between research freedom and institutional politics. A frontier refuge may prioritize privacy, mutual aid, and practical repair over formal ceremony.

Not every station needs every district or service. Distinctiveness comes from meaningful combinations and trade-offs, not from making every location a miniature capital city.

## Background life and station continuity

See [Station Life and Background Activity](STATION_LIFE_AND_BACKGROUND_ACTIVITY.md) for a deeper contract covering daily rhythms, resident roles, optional activities, visible supply, local events, and bounded persistence. This companion document expands the lived-in-station promise without requiring every resident or ambient detail to be simulated.

## The station continues when the visitor leaves

Stations should have a bounded but coherent world rhythm. Ships arrive and depart, people change shifts, goods are consumed and replenished, systems need maintenance, schools hold classes, public events begin and end, and local decisions have consequences.

The first design should distinguish:
- **Authoritative state:** facts that affect access, ownership, safety, supply, mission outcomes, or persistent consequences.
- **Simulated activity:** bounded processes whose outcomes matter to the station's state.
- **Ambient life:** visible and audible activity that communicates a living place without requiring every person or event to be deeply simulated.
- **Authored moments:** specific encounters, performances, demonstrations, or stories placed in the station's rhythm.

This separation keeps the world believable without requiring a full simulation of every resident. A public concourse can feel busy through ambient crowds and scheduled activity while the handful of people involved in a consequential conversation have persistent identities and relationships.

## Learning districts belong to ordinary station life

Schools and academies should be visible institutions, not hidden tutorial menus. A station may have a public science gallery, a navigation school, an engineering apprenticeship deck, a medical training center, a library, or informal workshops run by experienced residents.

Visitors might watch a class demonstrate orbital mechanics, help a learner understand a harmless systems fault, meet an instructor who later becomes a mentor, or discover a profession through a public exhibition. These moments can be optional and woven into the place. Learning is not automatically a mission, and a learner's future is not predetermined by the institution they first visit.

Training facilities can provide structured practice and qualification for specific responsibilities. Their standards should be credible: a simulator can teach and assess a skill, but authorization for hazardous real-world duties requires explicit qualification and supervision.

## Docking as a social transition

The passage from ship to station is an experience boundary with physical and social meaning. Depending on the technology and culture, it may involve an airlock, docking tube, gangway, hangar floor, passenger bridge, pressure equalization, environmental checks, identity verification, customs, quarantine, or local access rules.

The visitor should understand what is happening and why. Procedures can vary by station, faction, vessel, and situation. A trusted local pilot may receive a streamlined welcome; an unknown freighter may be inspected; a capital ship carrying a powerful figure may trigger ceremony, security, political attention, or public excitement.

The station should remember arrivals and departures where they matter. A vessel's reputation, ownership, markings, recent actions, or faction affiliation can influence clearance, service, welcome, suspicion, or denial. These responses should follow explicit world rules rather than arbitrary scripted hostility.

## First useful station slice

A first authored station should be small enough to understand but complete enough to demonstrate the promise:
- one recognizable approach and traffic-control sequence;
- at least two berth scales or vessel classes, so docking is not one generic animation;
- one public arrival route from berth to a shared concourse;
- a small set of distinct spaces, such as a market, repair/workshop area, learning space, and public observation area;
- a handful of persistent characters with different roles and relationships;
- one visible station rhythm, such as shift change or scheduled arrivals;
- one local circumstance that affects services or access;
- a meaningful departure that reflects what happened during the visit.

The slice should prove a connected arrival-to-life-to-departure experience. It should not claim to simulate an entire city or population.

## Questions requiring illumination

- What station scale and district set form the first useful vertical slice?
- Which docking actions are manual, assisted, automated, or delegated to station control?
- How do docking permissions, berth reservations, traffic control, and vessel classes interact?
- Which station services are simulated as authoritative systems, and which are initially represented as authored interactions?
- How do station traffic, population, supply, and local events evolve over time?
- How do security, customs, quarantine, and privacy differ across cultures and jurisdictions?
- How does a station remain useful and interesting when the visitor has no active mission?
- Which parts of station life need persistent simulation, and which can be communicated through bounded ambient activity?
