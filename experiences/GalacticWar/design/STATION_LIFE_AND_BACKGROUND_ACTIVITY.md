# Station Life and Background Activity

**Status:** candidate design contract; not approved world canon.

A station should be enjoyable before it offers the visitor a mission. It is a place where people have reasons to be, work to do, relationships to maintain, things to celebrate, and problems that do not exist solely for the player's benefit.

The goal is not to simulate every resident continuously. The goal is to make the station's life coherent, observable, and occasionally participatory. Background activity should make the world feel active while leaving room for the visitor to choose what deserves attention.

This document complements [Space Stations and Docking](STATIONS_AND_DOCKING.md), which defines the arrival-to-departure experience, and [Vesper Reach](FIRST_STATION_CANDIDATE.md), which proposes a first station where these ideas can be explored. Learning institutions and pathways are described in [Learning and the Living Social Galaxy](LEARNING_AND_SOCIAL_GALAXY.md) and [Learning Progression and Professional Qualification](LEARNING_PROGRESSION_AND_QUALIFICATION.md).

## The station has a day, even in space

Each station should have a recognizable rhythm appropriate to its purpose and culture. A useful rhythm can include:

- shift handovers in traffic control, engineering, security, medicine, and cargo;
- scheduled passenger arrivals, freight transfers, maintenance windows, and departures;
- classes, demonstrations, apprenticeships, research briefings, and public lectures;
- meal periods, markets, recreation, performances, community gatherings, and quiet hours;
- inspections, cleaning, repairs, inventory checks, and infrastructure maintenance;
- civic meetings, visiting delegations, ceremonies, celebrations, and memorials.

The station's clock need not map to a universal 24-hour day. Local schedules can reflect rotation, lighting cycles, work agreements, cultural practice, and operational needs. Public displays, lighting, announcements, queues, and resident behavior should help visitors read the rhythm without requiring a schedule screen.

A schedule is a framework, not a rigid parade of identical animations. Activity can vary with staffing, supply, emergencies, local events, and the station's current condition.

## Background activity should tell stories

Ambient activity is most valuable when it communicates something about the place. A cargo team arguing over a delayed manifest tells us more than generic workers walking past. An instructor adapting a lesson because a component shipment is late connects education to the station's economy. A mechanic closing a bay after a safety fault shows that procedures have consequences.

Examples of useful background scenes:

- tug crews guide a damaged freighter into a repair berth while traffic control clears its approach;
- a class gathers around a transparent model of the station's orbital path;
- a market stall changes its display because a supplier shipment arrived;
- two technicians disagree about whether to replace or rebuild an obsolete component;
- a public announcement invites residents to a festival, then later reports a venue change;
- a medical team receives an incoming rescue craft;
- apprentices watch a capital ship docking and ask the berth crew questions;
- a quiet observation gallery becomes crowded during a rare planetary event.

Scenes should have causes, context, and readable outcomes. Avoid repeating conspicuous activity without variation or presenting random busyness as proof of life.

## People need roles beyond waiting for the player

Residents should have understandable reasons to be in a location. A small number of persistent characters can anchor the first station slice: a traffic controller, berth chief, instructor, apprentice, market operator, technician, medic, residential coordinator, or visiting crew member.

Each persistent character should have only as much simulated state as the experience needs. Useful dimensions may include:
- role, responsibilities, schedule, and current assignment;
- relationships, affiliations, trust, and relevant history;
- immediate needs or constraints that explain current behavior;
- knowledge they can reasonably possess;
- current location or activity when it matters to an interaction;
- commitments and consequences that should survive the visitor leaving.

Not every passerby needs a biography, inventory, or persistent identity. Ambient residents can be generated or represented as bounded activity, provided they do not impersonate a consequential character or create false claims about authoritative state.

Characters should not freeze their lives until the visitor approaches. They may be busy, unavailable, asleep, off shift, traveling, or dealing with something else. The visitor can wait, return, ask another person, or choose another activity.

## Activities that are fun without becoming quests

A station can offer low-pressure activities that reward attention and curiosity:
- observe docking operations from a gallery and learn how different berths work;
- browse a market whose offerings reflect real local supply;
- attend a public lesson, demonstration, performance, or civic discussion;
- try a safe simulator or workshop exercise;
- help a resident with a bounded practical task;
- compare conflicting accounts on a public information display;
- explore architecture, gardens, exhibits, ship designs, and local history;
- join a recreation activity or simply spend time with companions;
- watch a repair, fabrication, cargo, or research process unfold.

These activities should not all produce currency, experience points, reputation, or mission progress. Some are valuable because they teach the world, create a memory, introduce a person, or provide a pleasant pause. The player should be allowed to enjoy a place without optimizing every minute.

A task becomes a mission when its stakes, dependencies, or consequences justify that weight—not merely because an interaction has a completion prompt.

## Local economy should be visible

Station services should reflect what the station can actually provide. Supply is shaped by production, imports, exports, storage, transport capacity, demand, damage, and regional conditions.

A repair yard with scarce precision components may offer temporary workarounds, longer lead times, or referrals to another port. A school may postpone a laboratory exercise when replacement materials are delayed. A market may have plentiful local food but limited imported luxuries. A blockade can affect cargo, medicine, maintenance, and civilian routines as well as military readiness.

The first implementation need not simulate a complete economy. It should preserve a small set of meaningful facts:
- what the station produces or specializes in;
- what critical supplies it depends on;
- what is currently available, scarce, delayed, or restricted;
- which services are affected and how visitors can discover why;
- what changes when a shipment, repair, route, or local decision resolves the shortage.

Do not create arbitrary scarcity just to steer the player. If a service is unavailable, communicate the cause and, where appropriate, an alternative.

## Events should emerge from station circumstances

Events can be scheduled, locally triggered, or authored. Examples include a visiting research vessel, a public festival, a school exhibition, a labor dispute, a damaged route beacon, a medical emergency, a diplomatic arrival, or a sudden security restriction.

An event should have:
1. a cause or reason it is happening;
2. people or systems involved;
3. a visible footprint in the station;
4. optional ways for the visitor to observe or participate;
5. an outcome that changes the station only where the event's stakes justify it.

Not every event requires player intervention. Some should resolve without the visitor, and some should remain unresolved until the player or other world actors take meaningful action. The station must not imply that every problem is waiting for its hero.

## Persistence without simulating everything

Separate station life into four levels:

1. **Authoritative facts:** ownership, access, safety status, inventory commitments, credentials, mission consequences, and other state that must be correct.
2. **Bounded simulation:** schedules, supply changes, work orders, traffic flows, and events whose outcomes can affect authoritative facts.
3. **Ambient presentation:** crowds, background movement, distant work, announcements, and decorative activity that communicate life but do not independently grant rewards or alter important state.
4. **Authored encounters:** selected conversations, demonstrations, discoveries, and scenes with deliberate narrative or gameplay significance.

The levels may inform one another, but presentation must not silently become authority. A crowd animation cannot prove that a ship arrived; a decorative cargo container cannot establish ownership; an ambient announcement cannot grant a qualification.

When the visitor leaves, important commitments and consequences persist. Low-value ambient details can be regenerated or advanced in bounded steps. Returning visitors should recognize meaningful changes without requiring every chair, passerby, or meal to have been tracked.

## The visitor is one participant, not the center of the universe

The station should remain legible and active when the visitor is absent, idle, or pursuing something unrelated. Residents can have priorities that conflict with the visitor's plans. Services can close for maintenance. A teacher can be occupied. A public event can begin without waiting for the player.

The visitor may earn trust, offer help, contribute expertise, or change a local outcome, but should not become the automatic owner of every institution or the solution to every problem. Local people retain agency and competence.

This also protects the tone of the world: ordinary life is not a queue of disposable quest givers. It is the social fabric that gives danger, sacrifice, discovery, and victory meaning.

## First demonstrable background-life slice

For Vesper Reach, a small convincing slice could include:

- one visible shift handover in traffic control or the repair district;
- one scheduled arrival with a distinct vessel and berth procedure;
- one learning activity that continues whether or not the visitor joins;
- one market or repair service whose availability reflects a known supply fact;
- three to six persistent characters with distinct responsibilities;
- one optional public activity with no mandatory reward;
- one local event that changes a visible service or routine;
- a return visit where at least one consequential change is remembered.

The slice should be deliberately small. It proves that station life has causes, rhythms, and continuity without claiming a fully simulated population or economy.

## Open questions

- Which local clock and schedule conventions make sense for Vesper Reach?
- Which resident roles need persistent identities in the first slice?
- Which supply facts should be authoritative initially, and which can remain authored?
- How should events advance while the visitor is offline or elsewhere?
- Which ambient scenes can be safely regenerated without contradicting persistent facts?
- How can the station remain welcoming and discoverable without overwhelming the visitor with notifications or chores?
- What privacy and consent rules govern persistent relationships, conversations, and learner records?

The intended result is a station that feels worth returning to—not because it constantly generates tasks, but because life there continues.
