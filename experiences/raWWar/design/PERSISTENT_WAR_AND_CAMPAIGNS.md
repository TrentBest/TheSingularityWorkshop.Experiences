# Persistent War and Campaign Perspectives

**Design status:** established raWWar direction with implementation details still to be proven.

## One continuing conflict

The war is a single continuing condition across a galaxy-scale civilization, not a sequence of disconnected maps with reset economies.

The Empire spans many star systems. Strategic movement is not instantaneous: warp jumps require days to initialize. The delay matters because decisions, intelligence, logistics, political pressure, and local events continue while forces prepare to move.

An Imperial authority can issue demands such as conquest or tithe. A faction may obey or refuse. Obedience can bring rewards, but those rewards may arrive with escalating obligations. Refusal may preserve local autonomy while increasing political, economic, or military pressure.

The world should preserve consequences rather than merely narrate them:
- ownership and control of worlds can change;
- resources, supply, and industrial capacity can shift;
- faction and Imperial power can rise or fall;
- political relationships and legitimacy can change;
- people and institutions inherit the consequences.

The exact numerical rules, diplomatic model, and authority hierarchy remain to be specified and tested.

## Two campaigns, one world

### Commander

The player begins as the head of a faction. The strategic layer provides authority over policy, deployments, industry, logistics, alliances, and military objectives. The player can enter first-person situations to experience the consequences of those decisions.

The commander is not omniscient. Information arrives through institutions, reports, scouts, and people with their own knowledge and incentives.

### Enlisted

The player begins at the lowest practical level and encounters the war through orders, training, duties, squad life, equipment, and local consequences. Advancement comes through learning, qualifications, experience, performance, and trust—not a detached level counter.

The player may rise into leadership and eventually faction command. That change in authority should be earned and represented in the same world model.

## Shared simulation

Both perspectives must read and affect the same authoritative world state. They differ in viewpoint, information, available actions, and scope of authority—not in the underlying facts of the war.

A local soldier's work should connect to command intent, supply, readiness, and outcomes. A strategic order should resolve through the actual capabilities, resources, people, and time available to carry it out.

## Scale and presentation

The intended visual progression is:

**Galaxy → star system → world → faction → military formation → soldier → consequence.**

Each level should explain its relationship to the next. A galaxy map is not a decorative backdrop; it is a projection of real ownership, movement, supply, and political state as those systems are implemented.

## Archives and contested history

The setting's history should be discoverable through archives, colonies, derelicts, institutions, and surviving records. Sources may be incomplete, contradictory, biased, or mistaken. The Experience should distinguish what happened from what a particular faction claims happened.

A visitor should be able to compare accounts and discover history rather than being handed one infallible exposition dump.

## Open questions

- What are the Empire's constitutional and practical limits?
- What determines the cost, duration, and failure risk of warp initialization?
- How do supply, production, and reinforcements propagate across star systems?
- What constitutes faction legitimacy, rebellion, and succession?
- How do casualties, recovery, replacement, and civilian consequences persist?
- How are uncertainty and contradictory records represented in the authoritative world model?
